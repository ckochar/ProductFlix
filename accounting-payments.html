01 · THE PROBLEM

Payment problems rarely start at the payment.

Money movement sat at the end of a much larger operational chain: orders, provider work, approvals, invoices, disbursement requests, accounting entries and reconciliation. When those upstream states did not line up, the symptoms appeared downstream as payment failures, manual research, vendor delays and leakage.

The core product problem was not simply “make payments faster.” It was creating enough shared structure and controls that operations, accounting and provider teams could understand what happened — and why.

THE PRODUCT QUESTION
How do you make high-volume money movement fast enough for operations, controlled enough for accounting, and transparent enough to debug?
01Fragmented state

Operational status and financial status did not always move together.

02Manual reconciliation

Teams spent time proving that transactions matched the work that created them.

03Exception hunting

Failures required cross-system research instead of a clear resolution path.

04Provider friction

Delays and ambiguity degraded vendor experience and cycle time.

02 · SEE THE SYSTEM

I treated accounting as a product system, not a back-office feature.

The useful unit of analysis was the full transaction lifecycle. Every dollar had a business event that created it, a set of approvals that legitimized it, a payment state that moved it, and an accounting state that had to reconcile it.

Mapping those states exposed where product design could eliminate ambiguity instead of merely adding another operational report.

04 · THE PRODUCT

A control plane for operational money movement.

The product experience connected request validation, approvals, payment execution, reconciliation and exception handling into a more observable workflow. The design goal was not one giant accounting screen — it was making the right financial context visible at the exact point a user had to decide or act.

05 · KEY PRODUCT DECISIONS

Financial products are defined by the failure paths.

01

Why not optimize only for payment speed?

Faster money movement without better controls can move errors faster too. Cycle time had to improve without weakening validation and traceability.

02

Why make reconciliation part of the product?

Because a payment is not operationally complete when money leaves. It is complete when the financial record matches the business event.

03

Why focus on exception UX?

Happy-path automation creates value, but unresolved exceptions consume disproportionate effort. A reason + owner + next action turns failure into a manageable queue.

04

Why standardize across workflows?

Shared financial primitives reduce duplicated logic while still allowing policy variation at the workflow edges.

07 · WHAT I’D DO NEXT
01

Predict exceptions earlier

Use historical failure patterns to flag risky transactions before they reach payment release.

02

Make reconciliation event-driven

Reduce batch-oriented checks by updating financial state as source events occur.

03

Build provider intelligence

Surface cycle time, exception rate and quality signals by provider to improve routing and performance management.

04

Unify financial observability

Create one transaction timeline spanning operational event, approval, payment, accounting and reconciliation state.

THE TAKEAWAY

Payments are not a button.
They're a system of states, controls and trust.

The strongest financial infrastructure products make complex money movement feel boring — because the complexity has been designed into the system instead of pushed onto the user.
